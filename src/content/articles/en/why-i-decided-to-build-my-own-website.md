Over the years, I've had an online presence in different forms, but I'd never quite built a space of my own where I could bring together my interests, projects and experiences.

As a computer engineer, the idea of having a personal website had always appealed to me. Not so much as a professional showcase, but as a place to share what I do, what interests me and what I learn along the way.

Over time, I'd also accumulated projects and ideas that deserved more than being left in a folder on my computer.

That's how the decision to create **fernandoghg.com** came about.

## A Space of My Own, Without Unnecessary Complications

I didn't want to build a particularly sophisticated website or turn its maintenance into another job.

I was looking for something simple: a fast website, easy to navigate, that worked well on computers and mobile devices and could grow gradually.

I also wanted to keep a certain amount of editorial freedom. I wanted to write about technology, software development, video games, simulation or anything else I found interesting, without having to fit everything into a single subject.

The idea wasn't to publish constantly, but to write whenever I had something worth sharing.

Above all, I wanted to avoid making the website more complicated to maintain than the content itself.

## The Opportunity to Build It with Artificial Intelligence

There was another reason to start the project: experimenting with a way of developing software that is changing rapidly.

Artificial intelligence tools make it possible to approach programming, documentation and analysis differently. I wanted to see how far they could help me develop a complete project and, especially, where their limitations might lie.

I decided to use **ChatGPT to analyse requirements, explore alternatives and make decisions**, and **Codex to work on the project's code**.

That didn't mean handing over every decision.

From the beginning, I wanted to maintain a supervised process: reviewing proposals, checking results and approving changes before incorporating them into the main version.

AI could help develop the website, but the decisions about what to build, how to build it and what to publish would remain mine.

## Choosing Simplicity Is Also a Technical Decision

One of the first decisions was to avoid an unnecessarily complex architecture.

For a personal website containing pages and articles, maintaining an application server, a database or services that would add little value didn't seem reasonable.

I eventually chose **Astro and TypeScript**, with a statically generated website published through **Cloudflare Pages**.

The choice reflected several goals: good performance, straightforward maintenance, minimal resource requirements and low operating costs.

I also decided to prepare the website for Spanish and English from the outset, although that doesn't mean every article has to be published in both languages at the same time.

It wasn't about using the most impressive technology, but the technology that best suited my needs.

## A Website That Respects Its Visitors

Privacy was another aspect I wanted to consider from the very beginning.

I didn't need to know each visitor's browsing habits, display personalised advertising or introduce tracking tools to determine whether the website was serving its purpose.

That's why I chose not to use my own analytics, advertising trackers or unnecessary third-party services.

I also tried to minimise dependencies and avoid features that would require collecting personal data without a clear need.

This doesn't mean a website published on the Internet can operate without any technical processing of data by its providers. It means I wanted to limit that processing to what was necessary and explain the decisions transparently.

## More Than an Introduction Page

As the project progressed, it became clearer that the website shouldn't be limited to a homepage with a few personal details.

I also wanted to use it to document other projects.

One of them is **Aldarte**, a mood-tracking application I'm developing with particular attention to simplicity and privacy.

Another is **The Last Lighthouse Keeper: Worlds of Infinity**, an exploration and puzzle adventure I'm creating with Godot.

Both projects have something in common with this website: they are opportunities to learn, experiment and discover how artificial intelligence can help when used as a development tool.

I intend to share their progress, including the decisions that work, the difficulties and any changes of direction along the way.

## What I've Learned So Far

One of the first conclusions I've drawn from this experience is that using artificial intelligence doesn't remove the need to think carefully about software design.

In some respects, it makes defining requirements and checking results even more important.

These tools can propose solutions and generate code quickly, but that doesn't guarantee every decision will be appropriate.

While developing this website, we've had to review security, privacy, accessibility, internationalisation and deployment. These are aspects that can easily be overlooked when you're concentrating solely on making a page look good.

I've also confirmed something fairly simple: **for a small project, keeping things simple is usually a good strategy**.

## A Project That Will Keep Growing

fernandoghg.com already has a working technical foundation, but that doesn't mean it's finished.

A personal website is, to a large extent, an ongoing project. Its content will evolve as new ideas, experiences and projects emerge that are worth sharing.

In future articles, I'll explain in more detail why I chose Astro, how I organised my work with ChatGPT and Codex, the decisions I made about privacy and how I prepared the deployment.

For now, this first article explains where it all began.

I didn't simply want to have a website.

**I wanted to build a space of my own and use the process as an opportunity to keep learning.**
