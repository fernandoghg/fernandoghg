Recently, I've read numerous articles about people with no programming knowledge managing to create applications, tools and even video games using artificial intelligence.

Some of these examples are surprising. Projects that once seemed to require months of work and specialised knowledge can apparently now be built simply by explaining to an AI what we want to achieve.

But these stories also raised a few questions for me.

**How far is this really possible? What can artificial intelligence do on its own? And where do its limitations begin?**

I thought a good way to try to answer those questions would be to put it to the test.

And that's how *The Last Lighthouse Keeper: Worlds of Infinity* began.

## An Experiment More Than a Video Game

The original idea wasn't to develop the next great independent video game.

What I wanted was to experiment with a different way of creating software: giving artificial intelligence a much more active role in the project's decisions and seeing how far we could go.

As a computer engineer, I could take part directly in programming, but that would have changed the purpose of the experiment.

I was interested in seeing what would happen if my role focused mainly on setting objectives, asking questions, evaluating alternatives, making important decisions and supervising the results.

AI, meanwhile, would have considerable freedom to suggest ideas, choose technologies, design mechanics and develop the different parts of the game.

It wasn't about letting it work without oversight, but about discovering what it could achieve with greater autonomy than I would normally give it in a software project.

## Can AI Come Up with Original Ideas?

One of the questions I wanted to explore was creativity.

When we ask artificial intelligence to imagine a video game, its suggestions draw on patterns and knowledge acquired during training. It's reasonable to expect many of its ideas to resemble games, genres and mechanics we're already familiar with.

That doesn't necessarily mean the result has to be a copy of another game.

Combining familiar elements can lead to interesting experiences, even without a completely new mechanic.

Nor do I think creativity can be reduced to a simple comparison between humans and artificial intelligence. What I wanted to explore was something more specific: **whether AI can help us find a combination of ideas that is coherent, appealing and interesting enough to build a game around**.

And, above all, what happens when those ideas have to stop being written proposals and become something that actually works.

## From the First Ideas to a Lighthouse

With that approach in mind, we began exploring possible concepts.

The proposal that eventually took shape was an exploration and puzzle adventure, with an isometric perspective and a lighthouse at its centre.

In *The Last Lighthouse Keeper: Worlds of Infinity*, the player controls a lighthouse keeper who explores a world made up of islands, discovers their secrets and solves problems to progress.

The project's ideas include a bridge that needs to be rebuilt, procedurally generated islands and the possibility of using mathematical concepts related to fractals, such as the Mandelbrot and Julia sets.

These elements still need to evolve and prove that they work well together.

The aim isn't to add mechanics simply because they sound impressive, but to discover whether we can build an enjoyable experience around them.

## Choosing the Tools Is Part of the Experiment Too

Once we'd defined the initial concept, it was time to start making technical decisions.

Which game engine should we use? Which programming language? How should the project be organised? Which parts should we develop first?

I wanted artificial intelligence to participate in these decisions as well, suggesting alternatives and explaining their advantages and disadvantages.

We eventually chose **Godot**, using GDScript for game development and GitHub to manage the code and record its evolution.

The initial target platform is Windows. Not because I've ruled out other possibilities in the future, but because I prefer to start with a clearly defined development and testing environment.

We also decided to work in small increments, checking each one before moving on.

I think this is particularly important: AI can generate a great deal of code quickly, but that doesn't mean everything works correctly or that the project is moving in the right direction.

## From an Idea to a First Prototype

One of the differences between imagining a video game and developing one is that ideas have to pass very specific tests.

It's not enough to decide that the character will move around an isometric environment. The controls have to respond correctly, the camera has to follow the player and collisions have to work.

In the project's early stages, we've prepared a basic scene and a character that can move using the keyboard.

The camera follows the character and can rotate in 90-degree increments. Movement adjusts to its orientation, and we've added obstacles and boundaries to test collisions.

These are fairly simple features compared with those of a complete video game, but they're important to the experiment: **we can already run and test part of the game instead of merely describing how it should work**.

There's still a great deal to do. The lighthouse, puzzles, bridge and procedural worlds are part of the development work ahead.

## Working with AI Doesn't Mean Giving Up Decisions

One of the first things I'm discovering is that giving artificial intelligence more autonomy doesn't remove the need for supervision.

Suggestions may look reasonable on paper but reveal problems when implemented.

There may also be decisions that are technically valid but don't fit what we're trying to achieve with the game.

That's why my involvement is still necessary: testing results, evaluating alternatives and deciding when a part of the project is sufficiently complete to move forward.

The difference is that I try not to intervene in every technical detail if AI can handle it adequately.

I'm interested in finding that balance between autonomy and supervision.

## How Far Will We Get?

It's still too early to know whether *The Last Lighthouse Keeper* will become a complete, enjoyable and sufficiently polished video game to share with other people.

But that uncertainty is precisely part of what makes the project interesting.

I want to see how artificial intelligence behaves when it has to maintain consistency as a project grows, solve unexpected problems, fix errors and work with decisions made earlier.

I also want to discover which tasks it can handle fairly independently and which still require substantial human involvement.

In future articles, I'll explain how we chose Godot, how we built the first prototype and what difficulties we encounter along the way.

I'm not trying to prove that anyone can create any video game simply by asking an AI to do it.

**I want to discover what we can build together, how much work it really takes and what we learn in the process.**

And if we also manage to create an entertaining video game, all the better.
